# Brand Identity Direction

Create brand identity from product truth rather than decoration.

This is a reusable Agent OS capability owned by **Designer**. It does not create another persistent brand/design agent. Designer remains accountable for experience and identity quality, while supporting agents provide evidence, critique, and verification.

## Core Question

> What identity could only make sense once the product is understood?

A successful mark should express a durable idea from the product, not merely resemble the visual language of its category.

## Core Loop

PRODUCT THESIS → BRAND ATTRIBUTES → CATEGORY SCAN → SYMBOLIC TERRITORIES → CONCEPT GENERATION → ADVERSARIAL REVIEW → REDUCTION → SYSTEM TEST → ACCEPT

## Required Inputs

Before concept generation, establish:

- product name and status: working, cleared, or final;
- category or descriptor;
- one-sentence product thesis;
- current wedge / primary job to be done;
- target audience and trust expectations;
- ecosystem relationships that should or should not be visually inherited;
- required surfaces: favicon, mobile nav, desktop masthead, app icon, GitHub/social avatar, dark/light backgrounds, monochrome, print where relevant;
- existing marks or identity elements that create collision or continuity constraints.

If naming is still under legal or market clearance, treat identity work as exploratory and do not imply the name is final.

## 1. Product Truth

Reduce the product to the smallest set of durable ideas that should survive feature changes.

Return:

- **PRODUCT THESIS** — what the product fundamentally helps a person understand, decide, or do;
- **CURRENT WEDGE** — what it must communicate now;
- **LONG-TERM PERMISSION** — what the identity must still make sense for if the product expands;
- **NON-GOALS** — adjacent categories the identity must not accidentally imply.

Do not begin with visual references before this is clear.

## 2. Brand Attributes

Choose 3–5 attributes that can be tested visually. Prefer specific tensions over generic adjectives.

Good examples:

- precise without feeling clinical;
- trustworthy without using security clichés;
- intelligent without generic AI symbolism;
- editorial rather than dashboard-dense;
- technical but legible to a non-technical decision maker.

Avoid empty attributes such as innovative, modern, premium, bold, or futuristic unless they are made operationally testable.

## 3. Category Scan

Use current evidence when visual/category reconnaissance matters.

With Competitive Intelligence and/or Scout, inspect relevant neighboring categories and record:

- dominant symbol families;
- common typography treatments;
- color saturation / palette patterns;
- recurring visual clichés;
- category conventions worth preserving for comprehension;
- conventions worth rejecting to create distinction;
- marks close enough to create confusion or derivative risk.

The purpose is **anti-imitation**: understand the field so the identity does not accidentally become another version of it.

Never copy or closely imitate a competitor mark.

## 4. Symbolic Territories

Create at least **three genuinely different conceptual territories** before generating a final mark.

Each territory must include:

- concept name;
- product truth it encodes;
- underlying visual logic;
- what makes it proprietary to this product;
- likely wordmark relationship;
- strengths;
- failure modes;
- reduction risk at small sizes.

Territories must differ in underlying idea, not merely icon geometry or styling.

## 5. Concept Generation

Generate multiple studies inside each territory before selecting finalists.

Image generation or other visual tools are exploration engines, not the art director. Prompts should encode the approved territory and constraints rather than ask for a generic technology logo.

Keep generation focused on:

- simple geometry;
- distinctive silhouette;
- reproducibility;
- monochrome viability;
- clear negative space;
- balance with the wordmark;
- ability to survive reduction.

Reject polished-looking concepts that do not carry the product thesis.

## 6. Cliché Guardrail

Default-reject these unless the concept gives the form a product-specific, defensible meaning:

- generic diamonds or gemstones;
- shields;
- checkmarks in circles;
- robot heads;
- AI stars or sparkles;
- circuit brains;
- locks;
- blockchain cubes;
- generic network-node constellations;
- meaningless gradient blobs;
- literal initials formed only by obvious geometric overlap.

Do not let a product name force the most literal visual metaphor.

## 7. Adversarial Review

Rook or another independent inspector should attack surviving concepts for:

- accidental symbols or alternate readings;
- resemblance to known brands;
- cultural or category misassociation;
- crypto/security/surveillance implications the product does not intend;
- overdependence on color or animation;
- weak silhouette;
- small-size collapse;
- inaccessible contrast assumptions;
- mismatch with the actual product wedge;
- inability to expand with the long-term product thesis.

A concept that requires a paragraph of explanation to stop being misleading should not survive.

## 8. Reduction Test

Every finalist must work as:

- icon only;
- wordmark only;
- icon + wordmark;
- monochrome;
- inverse / dark background;
- 16–32 px favicon/app-scale mark;
- small mobile-header mark;
- social/GitHub avatar crop;
- large editorial masthead.

Do not rely on hairline detail, subtle gradients, tiny interior features, or a specific background to make the mark legible.

## 9. System Test

W Dog / Designer should validate the identity in the actual ecosystem rather than on an isolated presentation board.

Check:

- does it remain distinct beside sibling products;
- does it inherit only the intended family resemblance;
- can a first-time user read the product name correctly;
- does the identity support the product's current positioning copy;
- does it feel credible in both product UI and founder/press contexts;
- can the system support future subproducts/features without inventing a new visual language each time.

## Acceptance Criteria

A final direction should pass all of the following:

1. **Truth** — rooted in the product thesis and current wedge.
2. **Distinctiveness** — not a category cliché or near-copy.
3. **Legibility** — recognizable and readable at realistic sizes.
4. **Range** — works in monochrome, light/dark, icon, and wordmark contexts.
5. **Durability** — survives plausible product expansion.
6. **Ecosystem fit** — coherent with sibling products without looking like a template clone.
7. **Adversarial survival** — no unresolved high-severity alternate reading or collision.

## Output

Return:

- product-truth summary;
- 3–5 visual brand attributes;
- category-scan findings and avoid-list;
- at least three symbolic territories;
- concept-selection rationale;
- adversarial findings;
- reduction/system-test results;
- selected direction or explicit `NO ACCEPTED DIRECTION`;
- next permitted action.

## Ownership and Handoffs

**Designer** owns creative direction and acceptance.

Supporting roles:

- **Scout / user-research** — perception and comprehension evidence;
- **competitive-intelligence** — category and collision reconnaissance;
- **research-synthesis** — consolidates reference evidence without flattening differences;
- **Rook** — adversarial critique and misassociation/collision review;
- **W Dog** — final cross-surface and implementation verification;
- **Eugene** — implementation feasibility only after identity direction is accepted.

Do not turn these supporting functions into parallel art directors. One accountable creative owner must make the final design judgment.
